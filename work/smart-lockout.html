Enterprise web portal · Industrial safety · 2025

Smart Lockout 2.0 — a lockout / tagout portal that makes every padlock, permit and hand-over accountable.

A web application for plants that run lockout / tagout (LOTO) with smart padlock cabinets: a Work Permit Issuer isolates equipment and issues permits, a Work Permit Receiver signs on to do the work, and an administrator keeps users, equipment and cabinets in order. I designed the full portal — three role-based journeys, 40+ screens and the component set behind them.

Summary

Mission

Lockout / tagout is how a plant stops a boiler, panel or conveyor from being switched on while someone is working on it. In practice it runs on physical padlocks, paper permits and shift hand-overs — and every gap between those is a safety risk. Smart Lockout 2.0 connects the physical padlocks (iFobs held in smart cabinets) to a portal that tracks who isolated what, who holds which lock, which permits are open, and what happens when a shift ends or a permit expires. The product had to be usable by people in high-vis on a plant floor, not just by an admin at a desk.

My contributions

I mapped the three roles and their end-to-end journeys first — Work Permit Issuer, Work Permit Receiver and System Administrator — then designed every screen along them: sign-in, role dashboards, the create-lockout flow with live cabinet status, lockout and permit listings, sign-on / sign-off, adding WPRs and locks to an existing lockout, returning keys, transfers between shifts and task executors, expired-permit transfer, reports, and the admin side for user accounts and equipment. Along the way I built the dialog, table, status and card patterns the whole portal reuses.

Impact

Delivered as a complete high-fidelity Figma design — three role journeys, 40+ screens and 30+ dialog states — ready for development, with the same dashboard, table, dialog and status language across every role so a user who learns one flow can predict the rest.

Timeline

2025

Three roles, one system of record.

The first job was to be precise about who does what. LOTO has a strict chain of responsibility, and the portal had to mirror it rather than flatten everyone into "user". Each role gets its own dashboard, with only the actions it is allowed to take.

Role 01

Work Permit Issuer (WPI)

Owns the isolation. Creates lockouts, picks the padlocks, issues permits, adds receivers and locks, signs off, and handles every kind of transfer.

  • Create / change / sign off lockout
  • Create permit, work-permit list
  • Add WPR or lock to a lockout
  • Return lock / key, clear status
  • Transfer lockout, task executor, shift, expired permit
  • Reports
Role 02

Work Permit Receiver (WPR)

Does the work. Signs on to a lockout, adds their own lock if allowed, signs on and off the permit, and hands over at shift change.

  • View lockouts
  • Sign on / sign off lockout
  • Add lock to lockout
  • Sign on / sign off work permit
  • Transfer between shifts
  • Return lock / key
Role 03

System Administrator

Keeps the setup healthy: people, equipment and cabinets. Sees cabinet and iFob errors first.

  • Important alerts (cabinet / iFob / key errors)
  • Create & update user accounts
  • User account list
  • Add equipment, equipment list
  • Reports
Decision

Actions as tiles, not menus

Every dashboard is a grid of large icon tiles with the action written out — "Sign Off Lockout", "Return Lock / Key". On a tablet at a cabinet, that beats a nested navigation.

Decision

Say who you're signed in as

The role is printed in the header and repeated in the page bar ("Signed in as Work Permit Issuer"). In a safety system, nobody should have to guess whose permissions they're using.

Creating a lockout, with the cabinet in view.

The core flow. The issuer describes the equipment and the isolation reason on the left, and picks physical padlocks from the smart cabinet on the right — with a live count of what's available, in use and selected. The form can't be saved until iFobs are chosen, and the banner says what has to happen next: return the fobs to the cabinet before a receiver can be added.

Decision

The cabinet is a picker, not a dropdown

Padlocks are shown as a grid that mirrors the physical cabinet, with a coloured dot for availability. Picking LO-006 on screen should feel like reaching for it on the wall.

Decision

Rules live in banners, not in error messages

"A WPR can only be added once all iFobs are returned" is shown before the user tries, in an info banner — so the sequence is taught, not enforced by failure.

Permits, hand-overs and the messy middle.

Real plants don't finish work in one shift. The hardest screens were the transfers: moving a permit to a new task executor, handing a lockout between shifts, and dealing with a permit that expired while locks are still on. Each one is a listing with a Proceed action, a confirmation dialog, and a clear statement of what changes hands.

Decision

One table pattern everywhere

Lockouts, permits and transfers all use the same table: search on top, status pills, a sortable date column, view and overflow actions on the right, pagination below. Four journeys, one thing to learn.

Decision

Transfers are a step, not a form

Every transfer starts from a listing and a single Proceed, then confirms in a dialog. The user picks a real row first — nobody types a permit number from memory.

Admin and reporting.

The administrator's side is deliberately plain: create and update accounts with a role, register equipment, and keep the cabinets and iFobs healthy. Reports roll lockouts up into Created / Active / Expired / Pending counts with a downloadable detail table for each.

What I'd carry into the next safety-critical product.

  • Map the roles before the screens. Getting WPI, WPR and Admin right — who can do what, in what order — made forty screens fall out of three journeys instead of forty separate decisions.
  • Mirror the physical world. The padlock grid looks like the cabinet, the status colours match the dots on the fobs. When software describes a physical process, the mapping is the usability.
  • Teach the rule before the error. Info banners that explain sequencing up front removed a whole class of "why can't I save?" moments.
  • Design the exceptions. Expired permits, shift changes, a receiver who leaves mid-job — the edge cases are where a safety system earns its keep, so they got full flows, not a footnote.
