Notebook AI - a paid proof-of-concept, and the effort estimate that priced the full build.

Notebook AI is an AI mentor for grades 9-12: ask it anything about your future and it turns the answer into a roadmap, saved in one notebook that remembers every chat. Before any of that could be built, the client needed to see it and know what it would cost. I designed a scoped proof-of-concept - marketing site, product UI, design system and gamification spec - and paired it with a screen-by-screen effort estimate for the full build, as a paid Upwork engagement.

Summary

Mission

A student in grade 9 to 12 has real questions - CS or design, how to build a portfolio, whether to take AP CS - and nowhere built for them to ask. Notebook AI's pitch was an AI mentor that answers in plain language and turns that answer into a roadmap with dates and a first step, saved automatically in a notebook it never forgets. The client needed this proven out on screen and priced before committing budget, so the brief was a proof-of-concept: convincing enough to sell the vision, and precise enough to scope the build.

My contributions

I designed the marketing site end to end - hero, the "ask, plan, achieve" loop, the notebook product shot, the progress and gamification proof, social proof and the closing CTA - plus the notebook product UI itself: sidebar notebooks, the auto-saved page, the AI mentor panel and the roadmap timeline. Alongside the screens I wrote the design foundations (colour, type, core components) and a full gamification specification - medal tiers, XP bar behaviour, streak states, logo animation timing and a motion token table - so engineering had exact numbers, not vibes. I then broke the entire product into modules by user journey and built the effort estimate from that breakdown.

Impact

Delivered as a paid POC: a convincing, on-brand proof of the product plus a clear, itemised number the client could take a decision on - with the design foundations and gamification spec ready to carry straight into the next phase of screens if the build goes ahead.

Design foundations - restrained structure, six saturated hues.

Layout, type and spacing stay as disciplined as any B2B SaaS product; colour and motion carry the "built for teenagers" energy. Every colour pairing was checked against WCAG AA before it shipped in the file, and the two brightest hues - lime and magenta - are scoped to fills and large text only, never small body copy.

Decision

Contrast guardrails, written down

The file states exactly where lime and magenta are allowed - fills only, headlines 24px and up - so engineering never has to guess whether a colour pairing is safe to reuse.

Decision

A mark that survives a favicon

One closed silhouette instead of an open, detailed illustration, so the logo still reads at 16px and works as a flat one-colour stamp - the point where most AI-startup logos fall apart.

A gamification system specified in numbers, not adjectives.

Ten levels at 500 XP each. Asking a question is worth 10, finishing a roadmap step 50, a daily challenge 30, a streak day 5 - and medals sit at levels 2, 4, 7 and 10, one geometry rendered in four materials so the set reads as a family. Every earning rule is written next to the artwork, because a badge without a rule is just a sticker.

Decision

Streaks fail quietly

A broken streak desaturates over 400ms rather than flashing a warning. The spec is explicit about why: shaming a teenager is how a habit product loses them.

Decision

Five motion tokens, not fifty

Every animation in the product maps to one of five named durations and eases - instant, fast, base, slow, spring - so engineering builds a small motion library once instead of guessing per screen.

Why this made the number trustworthy: the estimate wasn't a guess against the whole app - it was a sum of ten priced modules the client could see, cut or reorder. Scoping it against journeys already designed in the POC made the number feel earned rather than padded.

What I'd carry into the next AI-product pitch.

  • Design the mechanic before the manual. A level ring showing "340 XP, Gold unlocked" sells a gamification system faster than a paragraph explaining one would.
  • Write the rule next to the artwork. A medal with no earning rule is a sticker. Stating the rule in the same file as the icon is what makes it a system engineering can build from.
  • Price against work the client has already seen. An estimate anchored to journeys approved in the POC reads as scoped; one pulled from a vague spec reads as padded.
  • Map the loop before you map the screens. The flow diagram came first - every module in the estimate exists because it serves one step of that loop.
