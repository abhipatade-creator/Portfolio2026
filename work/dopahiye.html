E-commerce · Two-wheelers · Web · Outsourced · 2024

Do Pahiye — from "which bike?" to a booked test ride, without leaving the site.

A two-wheeler discovery and test-ride platform for India. Browse by brand, category, fuel type or budget, compare models with real ex-showroom prices for your city, and book a dealer test ride in three steps — with documents, payment and dealer confirmation handled in one flow.

Summary

Mission

Buying a two-wheeler in India is still mostly a showroom job: prices change by city, every brand has its own site, and a test ride means a phone call and a visit. Do Pahiye ("two wheels" in Hindi) was designed to move the whole front half of that journey online — discover, compare, test ride — and hand the buyer to a dealer only when they're ready to sign. The site had to work for a first-time scooter buyer and a Pulsar enthusiast comparing variants, on the same navigation.

My contributions

I designed the web experience end to end: information architecture and the four-way mega menu (brands, categories, fuel type, budget), the location-aware pricing model, the homepage and brand listing pages, product detail with specs and gallery, sign-in / sign-up with phone-number login and OTP, and the test-ride booking flow — from the login gate through vehicle selection, document upload, scheduling, payment and a post-booking status timeline the buyer and dealer both see.

Impact

The result is a single, consistent path from browsing to a confirmed ride, with the dealer relationship kept intact.

Discover — a mega menu that thinks like a buyer.

People don't shop for bikes one way. Some know the brand, some know the budget, some just want "a scooter under a lakh". The Explore Bikes menu opens with all four routes side by side, and the right-hand panel previews the model before you click through.

Decision

Preview before you commit

Hovering a model shows price, colours, "Add to compare", "Book a test ride" and "View virtual showroom" inside the menu. A buyer can shortlist three bikes without loading three pages.

Decision

Price follows the city

Ex-showroom prices in India vary by state. The location picker — city plus pincode, metro cities as photo tiles, other cities as chips — sits in the header so every price on the site is already the right one.

Select — listing pages built for comparison.

A brand page is grouped by model family — Freedom, Pulsar, Dominar, Avenger, Platina, CT — each with a launch banner and cards that carry the four things buyers compare first: starting price, colours, finance partners, and a direct "Book Now".

Decision

Family first, then variant

Grouping by family matches how people talk about bikes — "a Pulsar" before "an NS200". Each family gets its own banner and a "View all 12 Pulsar" link so the page stays scannable.

Decision

Finance on the card

Most two-wheelers in India are financed. Showing the partner logos on the card answers "can I get a loan on this?" before the buyer opens the product page.

Decision

Product page as a spec sheet

Overview tiles for performance and design, then features and a full specifications table, then gallery and reviews — with "Book a Test Ride" and "Buy Now" pinned at the top.

Sign in with your phone, not another password to forget.

The phone number is the login ID. Sign-up verifies it by OTP inline, checks the password rules as you type, and offers Google and Apple for people who'd rather not.

Test — book a dealer ride in three steps.

The test ride is where the site earns its keep, so it lives in a side panel over the product page rather than a separate journey. Three steps, a small booking payment to cut no-shows, and a status timeline that keeps the buyer informed while the dealer does their part.

Decision

Documents up front

Dealers need a licence and ID before they hand over a bike. Collecting them at booking — not at the showroom — is what lets the ride actually happen on the scheduled slot.

Decision

Honest about "pending"

The dealer has to accept the slot. Rather than pretend the booking is confirmed, the success screen says so plainly and the timeline updates when it is.

What I'd carry into the next one.

  • Navigation is the product. On a catalogue site, the mega menu does more selling than the homepage. Giving it four entry points and a live preview was the single biggest usability win.
  • Context first, then content. Asking for the city before showing prices avoids the worst e-commerce moment: falling for a number that turns out to be wrong for you.
  • Design the hand-off. The site couldn't replace the dealer, so the goal became a clean hand-off — documents collected, slot paid for, status visible — instead of pretending the dealer wasn't there.
  • Side panels keep momentum. Booking in a drawer over the product page means the buyer never loses the bike they were looking at.
