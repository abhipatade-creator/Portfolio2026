SaaS platform · Fintech · Outsourced · 2023

DigiAlly — a reliability score for every SME, and a way to find the ones worth backing.

A SaaS platform that rates Indian small and mid-size companies on a 0–1000 reliability score across ten dimensions, then lets investors and lenders drill from a national portfolio view down to a single company's report. I designed it from greyscale wireframes to the shipped interface, as an outsourced engagement through V2S Tech Solutions.

Summary

Mission

India has hundreds of thousands of registered SMEs and almost no comparable data on which of them are reliable partners, borrowers or investments. DigiAlly's answer is a score: every company rated out of 1000 across promoters, investors, associations, risk, social impact, employees, digitisation, cybersecurity, green index and suppliers, then badged Bronze, Silver, Gold or Platinum. The product had to make that score usable — to let someone managing a portfolio of hundreds of companies see where they stand, find the next ones, and read a full report on any of them without leaving the platform.

My contributions

I designed the web application end to end. First the information architecture: a portfolio dashboard on top, a geography → sector → industry drill-down underneath, company lists at every level, and a seven-section company report. Then greyscale wireframes for every screen so the client could agree structure and data before colour. Then the visual system — DigiAlly blue, score cards, badge tiers, the reliability gauge and dimension bars — and the high-fidelity screens that shipped, including a master-detail search view and the multi-section report.

Impact

The platform went live at digiallyglobal.com with the search, listing and report screens designed here. The drill-down structure meant a national view of 500,000+ companies stays navigable in three clicks.

Timeline

2023

From 500,000 companies to one, in three clicks.

The core design problem was scale. A treemap does the heavy lifting: the dashboard shows fund allocation by state, a state opens into sectors, a sector opens into industries, and every level carries its own top-rated, rising-star, newly-listed and at-risk lists so there's always something to act on.

Decision

Breadcrumb as the map

India › Maharashtra › Service › BPO sits above every drill-down page with a dropdown on the last crumb, so switching to a sibling never means going back up.

Decision

Same lists at every level

Top Rated, Rising Stars, Newly Listed and Risk Companies appear nationally, per state, per sector and per industry. Users learn the pattern once.

Making a number trustworthy.

A score out of 1000 is only useful if you believe it. The report page shows where it comes from: a gauge with the Bronze / Silver / Gold / Platinum bands, then all ten dimensions with their model weight and a graded bar, then the financial and business-model analysis behind them.

Decision

Badge and score travel together

The blue score card — number, "/1000", badge — is the same component on cards, list rows, the search panel and the report header, so a company is recognisable anywhere.

Decision

Show the weight, not just the grade

Each dimension's model weight (Low → Extremely High) sits beside its grade, so a low score on a low-weight dimension doesn't read as a red flag.

Search as a master-detail view.

Results on the left, the selected company's growth metrics on the right — revenue, profit, net margin and gross margin across quarters — with "View Full Report" one click away. Nothing selected yet? The panel says so instead of showing an empty chart.

More of the greyscale layer.

Structure was agreed in wireframes before any blue was applied — the treemap dashboard, the national top-rated view with emerging industries, and score-change tables that show old and new score side by side.

What I'd carry into the next data product.

  • Pick one navigation metaphor and repeat it. Treemap → treemap → list, with the same four lists at every level, meant nobody needed a tutorial.
  • A score needs its receipts. The gauge is pretty; the dimension table with weights is what makes an analyst trust it.
  • Make the key object a component. The score card appears in five places and never looks different — that consistency is what makes the product feel like one thing.
  • Wireframe the data, not just the layout. Agreeing which columns, which numbers and which states appear on each screen in greyscale saved every argument about it later.
